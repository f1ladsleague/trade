How it works, and why each part exists

BRIEFING FOR CONSTRUCTORS & DRIVERS
THE PROBLEM
Why the old flat fee had to go

Backmarkers were frozen out

A flat fee meant weak teams could never realistically prise a driver away. The market was dead.

Vetoes were a free destination-picker

Top drivers paid the same low fee whether dodging a disaster or blocking a sideways move.

Tiers went stale

Sameera sat on a Tier 2 label while running 2nd in the championship. Labels stopped matching reality.

THE PRINCIPLE
What a veto fee is actually pricing

A veto is you saying “I refuse to move.” The fee is the price of blocking that move — so it scales with what you would actually be losing.

PERFORMANCE-BASED

Your value comes from how you are driving this season — not your salary or an old tier label.

PROPORTIONATE

Dodging a disaster costs a lot. Blocking a sideways or upward move costs almost nothing.

BOUNDED

A floor so everyone can say no; a ceiling so nobody pays more than they could win back.

THE FORMULA
Three ingredients, two safety rails
1
YOUR VALUE
Sets your ceiling. Built from this season's points, finishes, podiums and discipline.
2
SEAT DOWNGRADE
How far the destination team sits below your current one — on banked points plus projected points.
3
TEAMMATE EFFECT
Your teammate is an ally. A stronger ally at the new team lowers your fee.

THE RAILS

FLOOR — 12% of your SLFEvery veto costs something. Blocking a trade is never free. CEILING — 80% of your SLFYou can never pay more than you could realistically win back.

Fee = clamp ( floor ,  Value × ( Seat Downgrade + Teammate Effect ) ,  ceiling )

WHY BOTH HALVES

Policy: loyalty is worth defending only where there is genuinely something to defend. A downgrade is measured by real competitiveness, not reputation.

INGREDIENT 3
The Teammate Effect — allies, not rivals

You win the Constructors' Championship WITH your teammate — never against them. So a strong teammate is an asset, not a threat.

Stronger ally at the NEW team

Moving becomes attractive → your veto costs LESS

Stronger ally if you STAY

Staying is valuable → your veto costs MORE

That ally is LOCKED IN

A safe bet → the effect counts in full

That ally is UNLOCKED

They may be traded away → effect discounted

Lock status matters far more for a STRONG ally than a weak one. Losing a title contender is a real risk; losing a backmarker changes nothing.

WORKED EXAMPLE 2
Mapa to Aston Martin — and why locks matter

Ferrari 625 → Aston Martin 579. Barely a downgrade — and Mapa would gain Nuwie G (143) as an ally, far stronger than Shiyan (84).

NUWIE G LOCKED IN

$57

Seat downgrade 0.32, ally bonus −0.19 → falls to the floor. A guaranteed strong ally makes the move genuinely attractive.

NUWIE G UNLOCKED

$98

Ally value discounted (143 → 90) because he might be traded away. The benefit is less certain, so the fee rises.

Rationale: locking a driver is a commitment, and the system rewards it. Certainty about your line-up makes your seat more attractive — and cheaper to fill.

THE RAILS
Floor and ceiling — fairness in both directions

FLOOR — 12% of SLF

Vetoing is never free.

Even blocking a sideways or upward move costs something. This stops frivolous vetoes and keeps the market moving.

CEILING — 80% of SLF

Never more than you can win back.

Nobody is asked to pay more than their championship payout is worth. Sameera's veto caps at $100 against his $125 SLF.

Together these guarantee the fee is always affordable, always meaningful, and never irrational.

IN ONE SENTENCE

Your veto costs more the better you are driving and the worse the team you are being sent to — discounted if you would join a stronger, locked-in teammate — with a floor of 12% and a ceiling of 80% of your prize money.

Forced to the worst team?
You pay near your ceiling.
Offered a sideways or better move?
Just the floor.
Joining a stronger locked-in ally?
Cheaper still.
A backmarker offered a top seat?
Barely anything — you would want to go.
